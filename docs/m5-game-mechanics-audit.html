M5 Game-Mechanics Audit

An honest structural review: where the game delivers on "accidentally learn D&D while learning to type," and where it will hit a wall.

What this is

A no-punches-pulled audit of the game's mechanics as they exist after the M5 playtest-fix batch (2026-07-09), written against the design docs (game design, content plan v2, the tuning pass, the M2/M3 scope and implementation docs including their playtest logs) and the shipped numbers in src/config/, src/engine/, and src/content/. Every claim below cites a file or a doc; the computed numbers use the committed config values as of this writing.

The brief it audits against is the project's own double promise: (a) be a typing trainer that stays fun for one specific 10-year-old over a long horizon, and (b) be true enough to D&D — in lore and mechanics — that playing it quietly teaches the real game. Those two goals mostly reinforce each other here, but where they fight, this doc says so plainly.

The verdict in three sentences

The core invention — two typists racing independent clocks, with a visible, fallible monster typist — is genuinely excellent and better than anything in commercial typing tutors; the D&D skeleton grafted onto it (abilities, the encounter d20, real 5e leveling, 4d6-drop-lowest creation, real Monster Manual names and CRs) is the right skeleton. The structural risk is that every single progression system — XP, coins, gear, content, story — is chained to one ladder (dungeon tier), and that ladder is chained to raw WPM, which for a real 10-year-old grows on a timescale of months-to-years with long plateaus; when his WPM plateaus, every meter in the game stops moving at once, and the only thing the game offers during a plateau is replaying the same dungeon. The second-order problem is legibility: the D&D-visible numbers (the d8, the +3) are dwarfed by hidden multipliers (length ×17, speed ×2), so the dice he's supposed to be learning from are, today, decoration on a formula he can't see.

Part II — The wall: three timescales pretending to be one

The game runs three progression clocks and currently welds them together:

  • Typing skill (the real thing being trained) grows on a calendar timescale — months per 10 WPM for a child, with long visible plateaus. It cannot be ground.
  • Character progression (XP → levels → ASIs, coins → gear) grows on a play-time timescale — kills per level. It can be ground.
  • Content progression (dungeons, monsters, story, text tiers) is a fixed 11-rung ladder consumed once.

The tuning pass deliberately pegged clock 2 to clock 3 (XP per kill is indexed by dungeon, config/rewards.ts's normalPerDungeon — farming D1 at 5 XP/kill can never meaningfully out-level a D5 wall that wants 14,000+ XP), and clock 3 is pegged to clock 1 (each dungeon's WPM band, config/dungeon-tiers.ts). That pegging was done for a good reason — it's what fixed the "over-leveled slow typist beats everything" inversion — but its side effect is the wall: when WPM plateaus, XP, gear, content, and story all freeze together. A D&D campaign survives a rough patch because the DM adapts and the story moves anyway; this game currently answers a plateau with "replay the Desert again."

Concrete math on the ladder's far end: the on-track anchors (content-plan-v2-tuning §4) put D8 at 54–65 WPM, D11 at 85–100 WPM, and the "ahead" corner at 118 WPM. 100 WPM is a top-percentile adult typist. A motivated 10-year-old practicing for a year typically lands around 25–35 WPM; 60 WPM would make him a standout teen. On any realistic horizon, dungeons 8–11 — text tiers 12–14, the Kraken, the Tarrasque — are out of a kid's reach for years, while the tier range he'll actually live in (D3–D6, 19–49 WPM) holds only four dungeons of content.
2026-07-10 — the top of the ladder belongs to the parents, on purpose. This audit originally recommended compressing the WPM ladder so D11 was kid-reachable. The project owner's intent is better: the game should be fun and genuinely hard for parents too — a kid who watches his dad stall on the Frost Giant learns that struggling at his own tier is normal, and parents racing each other to 100% a dungeon is its own game. D9–11's adult-grade calibration stops being a bug the moment the game says out loud who it's for. The recommendation below is rewritten to serve that intent instead.

Recommendations (this is the most important section of the doc):

  1. Make the family the meta-game, explicitly. Each player brings their own machine or browser profile (decided 2026-07-10 — no in-game save slots needed), so the competition is social and over-the-shoulder; what the game must supply is something worth comparing. A hall of records panel at the Inn — this save's best WPM, dungeons cleared, dungeons 100%-ed, bosses felled, flawless fights — gives every player a scoreboard to read out loud across the room. That's the "compete to 100% it" loop for the adults and the "even dad has room to improve" display for the kid — and it makes D9–11's brutal anchors a feature with an audience instead of dead content. What still needs fixing for adults: the marathon prompt structure at tiers 12–14 (Finding 6's all-or-nothing 1,800-char passages punish adults too — chunking keeps the difficulty in sustained speed, not in one-typo-loses-twelve-minutes), and an in-game signal of intended audience per dungeon (even one line on the tier card: "for grown-up typists").
  2. Stretch the middle. The kid will live at D3–D6 for a long time. That's where content breadth (more monsters per roster, dungeon variants, story) pays off most — not at D10.
  3. Add progression that moves during a WPM plateau. Cheap, high-leverage options, all D&D-flavored:
    • A bestiary. save.monstersDefeated already exists and is barely surfaced. Render it as Monster Manual pages that unlock on first kill (name, CR, habitat, flavor, typing stats revealed) — a collection meter that moves every session, and it teaches the actual Monster Manual. Probably the highest motivation-per-line-of-code available.
    • A quest board at the Inn. Three rotating micro-goals ("defeat 3 Forest monsters," "win a fight without a miss," "open the real chest") paying coins/XP. Gives a session a shape besides "grind the ladder," and no-miss/accuracy quests reward the quality axis that damage math currently ignores.
    • Visible typing progress. The game trains WPM but never shows it improving — stats.bestWpm is stored and near-invisible, per-battle WPM is computed and dropped, and stats.battlesLost is still dead (CLAUDE.md gotcha). A one-panel "training record" at the Inn (best WPM, recent trend, per-key trouble spots — the keyboard component already knows the keys) would let him see the plateau bending, which is the actual antidote to plateau discouragement.

Part III — Findings

Finding 1 — The on-track pace assumes ~2–3 full clears of every dungeon

config/rewards.ts tunes a dungeon's normal kill so that ~100 of them bridge to the next dungeon's target level. A full clear is ~30 regulars (config/dungeon-generation.ts targets 26–34) plus boss (10 normals of XP) and a mimic — ~45 normal-equivalents. So staying on-track means full-clearing every dungeon roughly two-and-a-quarter times, or grinding well past "cleared" before the next tier is survivable. Concretely: a fresh character needs 300 XP for level 2; a complete D1 clear pays ~215 XP. The pacing model's answer to "what do I do next?" is usually "the same dungeon again," and dungeon regeneration reshuffles the graph but not the monsters, the text, or the stakes.

Verdict: revisit. Either cut the kills-per-dungeon unit to ~1.3–1.5 clears (a values edit in normalPerDungeon, re-simmed), or make second clears different: a "hard mode" re-clear (elite modifiers on regulars, +1 band on text, better payouts) would make the grind a choice with teeth instead of a treadmill. The quest board (Part II) also converts repeat visits into goal-shaped sessions.

Finding 2 — The first hours are the game at its most punishing, and the last hours at its most trivial

Both ends of the difficulty curve are inverted from where a kid's tolerance actually is:

  • Early: hearts come from level milestones — 1 + floor(level/5) (engine/character/modifiers.ts) — so levels 1–4, i.e. the entire first two-to-three dungeons at on-track pace, run on one heart: a 7–10-fight gauntlet where a single loss wipes the run. Meanwhile 3–5 chests per dungeon hold exactly one real chest, so the natural kid move (open everything) is usually a surprise fight, and one mimic loss is the run. A fresh save has zero coins, so the Potion-of-Healing escape hatch doesn't exist yet. This was flagged as a watch-item in the M2 implementation log ("1-heart fresh-save start") and never resolved — M3 moved hearts from Endurance nodes to level milestones and reproduced the identical problem.
  • Late: a Potion of Healing costs a flat 15 coins (config/items.ts) while kill income scales with tier (rewards.ts: ~12/kill at D5, ~19 at D9) — by mid-game one kill refunds a heart, hearts themselves have grown to 3–4, and the attrition/wipe threat that defines the early game evaporates exactly when the player could finally enjoy pushing against it.

Verdict: revisit, with priority. Front-load the heart curve (e.g. 2 at level 1, +1 at 3, 6, 10, 15 — same cap, different shape; one array in modifiers.ts), start a new character with one free Potion of Healing, and keep D1's mimic count at the generator floor. Price consumables by tier (the coinsForTier helper already exists) so late-game hearts stay a real resource. D&D fidelity note: 5e is most lethal at level 1 and the M3 scope explicitly rejected that lethality for per-battle HP — the hearts system should follow the same reasoning it used.

Finding 3 — Rolling high on the encounter d20 can be a damage penalty: the band/length inversion

Damage scales with raw character count (engine/damage.ts's lengthFactor), but the v2 curriculum deliberately makes tiers 4 and 6 shorter than their neighbors (tier 4 = whole keyboard in 3–5-word bursts; tier 6 = first caps/punctuation, short lines). Measured over the shipped corpus (content/text/library.json), average length by tier is 17, 27, 27, 19, 47, 42, 52… — non-monotonic at exactly the rungs a beginner plays. With the committed config (referenceLength 12, cap 20) the per-tier average lengthFactor is:

In the Forest dungeon (D2, band tiers 2/3/4) the consequences are exactly backwards: the high band serves tier-4 text at lengthFactor ≈ 1.57 — harder to type and ~28% less damage than the mid band's 2.17. A natural-1 fumble (low band, tier 2, ×0.75 cap) computes to 2.17 × 0.75 ≈ 1.63 — a fumble out-damages a high roll in D2. In D1, high band (tier 3) pays zero damage premium over mid for strictly harder text. Tier 5→6 inverts again in D4/D5's bands. Proficiency growth auto-pushes every character toward the high band, so leveling up walks the player into the anti-reward. The sim's win-rate corners never caught this because it washes out over whole battles — but the kid feels per-fight "I rolled great and hit like a noodle," and it quietly teaches "high rolls are bad," the exact opposite of the d20 lesson the game wants to teach.

Verdict: fix now; this is the closest thing to a bug in this list. Cleanest fix: stop deriving the tier reward from raw char count and give each text tier an authored damageFactor in config (monotonic by construction, tuned once by the sim); keep raw length only as a small within-tier wobble. Alternatively pad tiers 4 and 6 to their neighbors' lengths in the content pipeline — but that fights the curriculum, which is right to keep new-key tiers short.

Finding 4 — The dice are decorative: the D&D-visible math is ~5% of the damage

The kid sees a d8 roll a 6, plus a +3 STR mod. The engine then multiplies by damageScale 1.6 × lengthFactor (1.4–17.8) × speedBonus (1–2) — so the "9" he can reason about becomes 53 damage in D1 and 227 at tier 10, driven by numbers he never sees (engine/damage.ts). In real D&D, 1d8+3 is the damage — that legibility is the entire pedagogical point of dice. Here the roll is a mood, not a mechanic he can learn from; it also means weapon choice (the most tangible D&D decision the Shop offers) moves damage far less than which text band he rolled into.

Verdict: revisit — the deepest, most transformative change in this doc. Fold length into dice count instead of a hidden multiplier: a tier-2 line rolls 2d8+3, a tier-9 passage rolls 8d8+3, a boss passage is a fistful of dice — "bigger text = more dice" is instantly legible, reads like a fireball (8d6!), and makes every number on screen a real D&D number. Mechanically it's the same scaling (dice count ≈ lengthFactor, already capped), the crit rule ("roll them all again") becomes spectacular on big passages, and monster HP is already authored against the hit distribution by the sim, so re-authoring is a re-run. If that's too big a bite, the minimum fix is presentational: show the multiplication ([6+3] × 5 = 45) so the formula is at least visible — but the dice-count version is the one that serves both promises at once.

Finding 5 — Per-point ability value is wildly unequal; ASIs have a "correct answer"

Post-Story-2 magnitudes (config/abilities.ts): +5 STR is +25% base-hit damage — before the length/speed multipliers dilute it. +5 CHA cuts monster WPM 20% and monster accuracy 25% and discounts the shop 15%. A Kraken at 0.98 accuracy charmed to ~0.73 goes from one typo per 50 characters to one per four — with 0.3s attention and 1.08 slack, its attack cycle collapses. WIS at 1200ms/mod buys +6s per prompt at +5. The tuning doc's own behind-corner check (+5 WIS/+5 CHA → 98–100% win everywhere it measured) is the smoking gun: the linear tempo levers were buffed until they dominate, and a kid who asks "what should I raise?" now has one right answer that isn't any of the classic fantasy stats. Meanwhile the acknowledged open finding that the Wizard is the weakest class (D1 on-track 8%!) still stands. Rolled-stats creation makes this worse: a character who rolls high CHA/WIS is simply better than one who rolls high STR/DEX, and the kid can't know that when he picks.

Verdict: revisit via the sim, with an explicit parity target. Add a sweep that measures win-rate delta per +2 ASI in each ability, per dungeon band, and tune magnitudes until every ability's delta lands in one loose band (say, within 2× of each other). Likely moves: cap or diminishing-return the charm accuracy cut, make STR's bonus additive dice or flat damage after scaling (so it doesn't dilute), and give the Wizard its identity fix (Finding 8) rather than more encounter bonus. Build variety is a fidelity issue, not just a balance one — "there are many viable builds" is a core D&D truth the game currently contradicts.

Finding 6 — All-or-nothing submits don't scale to stamina tiers, and 15-minute bosses don't scale to 10-year-olds

Exact-match-or-miss is the right rule at 20–100 characters: legible, honest, skill-expressive. At tier 12–14 (700–2000 characters), one un-noticed slip means several minutes of typing pays zero damage and a 2-second "miss" pause — variance a learner experiences as theft. The tuning pass's own boss targets (6/10/12/15-minute battles, three flawless ~1800-char passages for the final boss) passed the sim because a simulated 0.92-accuracy typist doesn't have feelings, an attention span, or a bedtime. This is the clearest case in the codebase of the sim tuning precision onto a target no one has validated as fun.

Verdict: revisit before any player reaches tier 10+. Chunk long passages at sentence boundaries: the passage is the fight's script, each sentence is one attack with its own clock and its own dice. A boss becomes a multi-round set piece (very D&D — a boss is many rounds, not one save-or-die), variance drops to sentence-size, mid-fight progress is real, and battle length stops needing flawless marathon prompts. This also single-handedly fixes Finding 7. Ladder compression (Part II) makes the residual time targets humane.

Finding 7 — The campaign story is written, then shredded

The v2 content pipeline authors a real hidden campaign per dungeon — five story sections, a voiced cast (Mab the GM, Bront, Quill, Sable, Fife), pre-rolled dice with bad rolls written into the narrative. Then the game serves those lines randomly sampled from tier pools, so the kid types chapter fragments in shuffled order and the story is unreadable as story. The single richest lore asset the project owns — and the thing that could make him want to fight the next node ("what happens to Bront?") — is currently white noise. The plumbing gap was even flagged in the tuning doc (FightEncounter needs dungeon id + section) and left unproven.

Verdict: revisit — likely the best fun-per-effort in this doc after the bestiary. Serve section text in order within a node's fight (and sections in order along the path), with the boss serving the finale passage. Combined with Finding 6's sentence-chunking, a battle literally becomes reading the next scene of the campaign aloud through your fingers — which is the game's whole premise achieved.

Finding 9 — Smaller ledger items

  • Coins overflow at endgame — and the fix is sink shape, not more items. The catalog's variety is fine: 11 consumables already cover every axis the game has (damage, tempo, luck, defense, healing, hearts), and more SKUs would just dilute the kid's ability to know what things do. The gaps are structural: (a) weapons are one-time purchases and a single build only ever wants its own ability line (~1,000–1,500 of the ~3,270 catalog), (b) consumable prices are flat while kill income scales ~10× across the ladder, so the only recurring sink becomes free by mid-game, and (c) nothing is aspirational. Fixes in order of value: price consumables by dungeon tier (the coinsForTier helper exists); add a short top shelf of expensive permanents priced for the parent tiers — a +3 weapon line, a Bag of Holding (+1 active-buff capacity), a Cloak of Vitality (+1 heart cap) — a few thousand-coin purchases that give D9–11 income something to chase; and charge a token inn-rest fee (Finding 8). That's enough — resist inventing a bigger catalog.
  • Levels 16–20 are theoretical for a kid. L15→16 is ~570 D11 kills; the ASIs at 16 and 19 effectively don't exist on a child's horizon. With D9–11 owned as the parents' game (Part II), the top of the XP table becomes their chase content and can stand as-is — but it should be visible on the hall of records, not silent.
  • speedBonus saturates early (acknowledged in combat.ts's comments): past ~2× baseline the per-hit bonus barely moves; the real speed reward is attack rate. That's actually good design — but it means the damage formula's speed term is mostly vestigial and could be simplified when Finding 4 is addressed.
  • Accuracy is invisible. The game's damage math rewards speed and punishes error only via time; nothing anywhere celebrates a clean streak. A per-fight "flawless!" flag (zero misses) feeding the quest board / bestiary is nearly free and trains the habit that actually matters at his stage.
  • stats.battlesLost is still dead (CLAUDE.md gotcha) — wire it or drop it; a training-record panel (Part II) wants it.

Part IV — Bug ledger: where the code disagrees with its own intent

Added 2026-07-10 after playtest reports of two live bugs ("sometimes I can't type anymore," "lines start with a space"). Both are found and explained below, along with everything else a full source sweep turned up — every entry was traced through the actual code path before being listed; things that looked like bugs but turned out to be documented intent (the signed negative-CHA monster buff, additive crit ranges, buffs burning on a lost fight, the 5e table values) were verified and excluded.

The two reported symptoms, explained

  • "Pressing keys stops affecting the prompt" = lost focus, never recovered. The only real input is a visually-hidden sr-only <input> (ui/battle/PlayerPrompt.tsx:73-84) that is focused exactly once, via autoFocus on mount. Nothing anywhere refocuses it: one click on the monster panel, the on-screen keyboard (an irresistible click target for a kid), or empty space blurs it, and every keystroke after that goes nowhere. Because the input is invisible, there is also nothing to click to get it back. Fix: a click anywhere in the battle screen refocuses the input (onMouseDown + preventDefault on the panel), and the input's onBlur refocuses itself while the battle is ongoing.
  • "Lines start with a space" = the word-wrap carries the boundary space onto the next line. wrapPrompt (ui/battle/TypedProgress.tsx:52-85) preserves every character 1:1 (correct — indices must map to glyphs), but when a line fills up, the inter-word space token that didn't fit becomes the first character of the next line. The kid sees a wrapped line that visually starts one column in, and — worse — must type an invisible leading space before the first visible letter; typing the letter he sees paints a red error glyph. Fix: a whitespace token that overflows the line appends to the previous line's tail (a trailing space is invisible under overflow:hidden; the 1:1 index map is preserved), so a wrapped line always starts on a visible character.
